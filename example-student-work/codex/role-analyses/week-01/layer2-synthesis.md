# Layer 2 Synthesis

The three readings fit together as a chain from general security theory to nuclear-sector implementation. Anderson supplies the conceptual frame: security is adversarial, economically constrained, and shaped by human behavior. RG 5.71 converts that frame into a regulatory program requirement for nuclear licensees: identify digital assets connected to safety, security, and emergency preparedness functions, then protect those assets through a Cyber Security Program using defense in depth. NEI 08-09 fills in the industry implementation layer by describing how licensees determine Critical Digital Assets and organize controls across access control, configuration management, media protection, incident response, physical protection, system integrity, training, and supply chain.

The key technical claim across the readings is that cybersecurity cannot be reduced to perimeter defense. Anderson argues that attackers will move toward the cheapest successful path, including social engineering and process weaknesses. RG 5.71 responds with defensive architecture, separation, and programmatic controls. NEI 08-09 acknowledges that the real plant environment includes legacy equipment, safety constraints, and compensating controls when direct modification is impractical.

The most important assumption is that a licensee can identify which digital assets matter before applying the program. That assumption is necessary because plants differ, but it is also a gap. If the CDA determination process misses a dependency, pathway, vendor support channel, removable media workflow, or data path, the later control families may look complete while protecting the wrong boundary.

A standard LLM synthesis would likely add useful crosswalks that a first-time reader might miss. For example, it would probably connect Anderson's economics argument to RG 5.71's defense-in-depth model: the goal is not perfect prevention but raising attacker cost, increasing detection probability, and limiting consequence. It might also notice that NEI 08-09's compensating controls are not exceptions to security but examples of security engineering under operational constraints.

A standard LLM synthesis would likely flatten the nuclear-specific tension between safety and security. It might summarize "apply controls to CDAs" without emphasizing that some controls can be hard to apply to fragile, qualified, or vendor-supported systems. It might also understate the regulatory importance of documentation, inspection evidence, and traceability. In this domain, being secure in practice and being able to demonstrate program compliance are related but not identical tasks.
