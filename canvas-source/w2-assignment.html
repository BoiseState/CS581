W1 asked what an attacker could reach. W2 asks who would actually try, what they have done before, and what you can prove about them from public sources. The profile you build here is the adversary half of every workshop that follows.

Deliverable 1 — adversary-profile.json

Commit to workshops/week-02/adversary-profile.json. One nation-state actor, profiled in depth. Base every claim on publicly available reporting and do not speculate past what those sources support.

The file must parse as valid JSON. Use this schema exactly:

{
  "target_system": ["PPC"],
  "w1_map_reference": "workshops/week-01/attack-surface-map-ot.md",
  "pivot_path": "how this actor would move from your W1 system to your W2 system, or why they could not",
  "actor": "...",
  "also_known_as": ["..."],
  "attributed_to": "...",
  "confidence": "documented | assessed | theoretical",
  "provenance": "specific public report, with section or page",
  "motivation": {
    "primary": "...",
    "secondary": ["..."]
  },
  "actor_type": "nation-state | criminal | hacktivist | insider | accidental",
  "nuclear_targeting_evidence": "...",
  "primary_ttps": [
    {
      "tactic": "...",
      "technique": "...",
      "mitre_ics_id": "TNNNN or TNNNN.NNN",
      "nuclear_relevance": "...",
      "system_relevance": "which interface on YOUR W1 map this technique would reach",
      "confidence": "documented | inferred | theoretical"
    }
  ],
  "historical_precedent": ["..."],
  "detection_opportunities": ["..."],
  "gaps_in_public_knowledge": ["..."]
}

Six to ten entries in primary_ttps. Map each one to a MITRE ATT&CK for ICS technique ID.

A numbering trap worth knowing: the ICS matrix uses both T0NNN and newer T1NNN.NNN sub-technique numbering, so the prefix does not tell you which matrix an ID belongs to. Look the technique up in the matrix rather than inferring from the number. An Enterprise ID is acceptable only where no ICS technique applies, and you must say so in that entry.

Three fields carry the most weight. system_relevance is where W2 connects to W1: for each technique, name the specific interface on your own attack surface map that the technique would reach. gaps_in_public_knowledge is where you record the attribution claims that exist but cannot be verified from open sources. Professional threat intelligence always separates what is known from what is assessed, and an empty gaps array means the analysis was not finished. pivot_path is new: every one of you now has two systems in play, your W1 system and your W2 system. Name the interface this actor would use to move between them. If there is no path, say so and say why. That answer is worth as much as a path.

Deliverable 2 — threat-cards/

Commit to workshops/week-02/threat-cards/. Five additional actors, at stat-line depth rather than full depth. These are not the actor from Deliverable 1.

The point is breadth. One deep profile teaches you an actor. Five cards teach you the landscape, and a card format forces you to decide what actually distinguishes one group from another.

Your five must span at least three different actor_type values. Five nation-state groups is one type five times, not a landscape. The course adversary taxonomy is nation-state, insider, hacktivist, criminal, and accidental. Independent operators and small collectives belong here: different motivations, different resources, different time horizons, and they fail differently than a state programme does.

actor_type asks what kind of adversary this is, not how capable it is. Those are different questions and one field cannot answer both. Where a group's public presentation and its assessed attribution disagree, put the presentation in actor_type, the attribution in assessed_affiliation, and say so on the card. That disagreement is usually the most interesting thing about the actor.

Two parts:

  • cards.json, an array of five objects, one per actor: 
    {
  "actor": "...",
  "aka": ["..."],
  "actor_type": "nation-state | criminal | hacktivist | insider | accidental",
  "assessed_affiliation": "optional; only when presentation and attribution differ",
  "sector_targeting": ["..."],
  "signature_ttp": { "technique": "...", "mitre_ics_id": "TNNNN or TNNNN.NNN" },
  "first_observed": "...",
  "confidence": "documented | assessed | theoretical",
  "provenance": "named public source",
  "what_this_card_cannot_tell_you": "..."
}
  • Five card files, one per actor, named card-01 through card-05 with whatever extension your format uses.

Style is yours. Baseball card, Pokemon card, tarot, trading card, anime, whatever you find fun. The format is deliberately loose. Every card must legibly show the actor name, actor type, signature TTP with its MITRE ID, and a provenance line naming a public source.

How to make them. Either approach is fine, pick whichever you will actually enjoy.

Option A: ask your coding assistant to emit each card as SVG or HTML. Costs nothing, keeps the files small and diffable, and getting an agent to produce clean SVG is a useful exercise in its own right.

Option B: use an image generator. You are welcome to, and several produce very good card art: ChatGPT, Nano Banana in Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot or Designer, Adobe Firefly, Midjourney. Stick to US-based providers with clear data handling practices, consistent with the course policy on AI tools.

Do not buy a subscription for this assignment. Free tiers are more than enough and Option A costs nothing at all. Nobody gets a better grade for having paid for a tool.

Mixing the two usually produces the most readable card: generate the artwork as an image, then have your coding assistant lay the stat line and provenance text around it in HTML or SVG. Generated images tend to mangle small text.

One hard rule: no card may depict a real person. Original characters, mascots, creatures, emblems, and abstract art are all fine. What is not fine is art that presents itself as showing what a real operator looks like. Several of the actors you will profile are tied to named individuals under federal indictment, including in the CISA advisory on this module's reading list. Inventing faces for them is the one thing this assignment will not accept.

Cards are graded on whether the stat lines are accurate and sourced, not on artistic quality. A hand-drawn card photographed with your phone scores the same as a polished render if the data is right.

Deliverable 3 — session-log.md

Commit to workshops/week-02/session-log.md. Full AI session log for all W2 work, graded independently.

Include a short reflection at the end on the card exercise. You will be asking a generative tool to produce confident, attractive artifacts about actors whose attribution is contested and in some cases disputed by the accused. Write down what that felt like and what you would want a reader of those cards to know.

Ground the reflection in Anderson’s Psychology and Usability chapter (3rd ed. Ch. 3, 2nd ed. Ch. 2). That chapter is about cognitive exploitation, authority compliance, and why people believe things that are presented well. Your cards are a specimen of exactly that: a polished card with a stat line and a MITRE ID reads as more authoritative than the same claim in a sentence, even though the evidence behind it has not changed. Address at least what the card format added that the source did not support, and which Anderson concepts explain why it persuades past its evidence.

Module 9 returns to this question about AI in nuclear operations, and your answer here is the start of that thread.

Deliverable 4 — synthesis.md

Commit to role-analyses/week-02/synthesis.md in your secondary repo. One page, read through two lenses: Nation-State Threat Analyst and Insider Threat Investigator.

The divergence to look for: the threat analyst thinks in external actors, technical TTPs, and attribution. The investigator thinks in organizational psychology, behavioral baselines, and the limits of technical detection. Apply both to your own profile and cards, not to the reading in general.

Anderson grounding, required. The Insider Threat Investigator lens must ground its behavioral-indicator claims in Anderson’s Psychology and Usability chapter (3rd ed. Ch. 3, 2nd ed. Ch. 2), citing at least two specific concepts from the chapter. Name them. "Anderson discusses psychology" is not a citation. That chapter is the investigator's entire evidentiary basis: what conditions create insider risk, and which indicators are actually observable rather than merely plausible. The threat analyst works from technical TTPs. The investigator works from this, and that difference in evidence base is where the two roles genuinely diverge.
