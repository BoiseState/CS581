Step 1 of 7

Choose your actor

One nation-state actor, profiled in depth. The choice matters less than your ability to source it.

Where actor names actually come from

Do not ask your assistant to invent a list. Start from primary sources and derive names from them:

  • MITRE ATT&CK for ICS, Groups — the closest thing to a standardised catalogue, and every entry carries technique mappings you will need anyway.
  • CISA advisories and joint alerts — including AA22-083A on this module's reading list, which covers indicted state-sponsored actors targeting the energy sector.
  • Vendor threat-group reporting — Dragos, Mandiant, and similar. Useful, but note that vendors name groups differently and the naming does not always line up.
The naming problem is part of the lesson. The same activity cluster often carries three or four different names depending on who is reporting. Your also_known_as field exists because of this. If you cannot reconcile the names, say so in gaps_in_public_knowledge.
Attribution is contested, and you are not resolving it. Your job is to record what public reporting claims, who claims it, and how strongly. An actor that a government has formally indicted is on different evidentiary footing than one a vendor has named in a blog post. Your confidence field should reflect that difference, not flatten it.

Step 2 of 7

Build adversary-profile.json

Structured output is the capability this module tests. The file must parse.

The prompt

Adapt this. Do not paste it unchanged and accept what comes back.

Three fields carry the most weight

FieldWhat it is for
system_relevanceOn every TTP, name the specific interface on your own W1 map that the technique would reach. This is the field that connects W2 to W1. A generic answer here means the profile is about nuclear facilities in general, which is not the assignment.
pivot_pathHow this actor would move from your W1 system to your W2 system. Name the interface. If there is no path, say so and say why. Every one of you now has two systems in play, and this is where they connect.
gaps_in_public_knowledgeAttribution claims that exist but cannot be verified from open sources. An empty array means the analysis was not finished.
Six to ten TTP entries. Map each to a MITRE ATT&CK for ICS technique ID. Note that the ICS matrix now uses both T0NNN and newer T1NNN.NNN sub-technique numbering, so the prefix alone does not tell you which matrix an ID belongs to. Check the technique in the matrix rather than inferring from the number. An Enterprise ID is acceptable only where no ICS technique applies, and you must say so in that entry.

Validate before you commit

Paste your adversary-profile.json here. This checks that it parses and that the required keys are present. It does not check whether your content is any good.

Step 3 of 7

The system_relevance pass

Go back through your own W1 attack surface map, one TTP at a time.

Why this is a separate step

If you fill system_relevance while the assistant is generating the profile, it will write something plausible and generic. Do it as a second pass, with your W1 map open, and the answers get specific.

Select your system on the first page to see your map file.

What good looks like. Not "could target the historian" but "would reach the store-and-forward buffer identified in row 4 of my OT layer map, which has no authentication on the collector side." The row number is the tell that you actually went back and looked.
If a technique has nowhere to land on your map, say so. A TTP with no reachable interface on your system is a real finding, not a failure. Record it, and consider whether your W1 map missed an interface or whether the technique genuinely does not apply.
Step 4 of 7

Threat cards

Five actors beyond the one you profiled. Stat-line depth. The point is breadth.

Style is yours

baseball cardPokemon cardtarot trading cardanimevaporwave field guideyour call

Every card must legibly show four things: actor name, capability tier, signature TTP with its MITRE ICS ID, and a provenance line naming a public source.

Spread the tiers. Your five cards must span at least three different capability tiers. Five nation-state groups is one tier five times, not a landscape. The course taxonomy is nation-state, insider, hacktivist, criminal, and accidental. Independent operators and small collectives belong here: different motivations, different resources, different time horizons, and they fail differently than a state programme does.

Cards are graded on whether the stat lines are accurate and sourced, not on artistic quality. A hand-drawn card photographed with your phone scores the same as a polished render if the data is right.

One hard rule

No card may depict a real person. Original characters, mascots, creatures, emblems, and abstract art are all fine. What is not fine is art that presents itself as showing what a real operator looks like.

Several of the actors you will look at are tied to named individuals under federal indictment, including in AA22-083A on this module's reading list. Inventing faces for them is the one thing this assignment will not accept.

Making them

Option A. Ask your assistant to emit each card as SVG or HTML. Costs nothing, files stay small and diffable, and getting an agent to produce clean SVG is a useful exercise in its own right.

Option B. Use an image generator. You are welcome to, and several produce very good card art:

ChatGPTNano Banana (Gemini)Copilot / DesignerAdobe FireflyMidjourney

Stick to US-based providers with clear data handling practices, consistent with the course policy on AI tools.

Do not buy a subscription for this. Free tiers are more than enough and Option A costs nothing. Nobody gets a better grade for having paid for a tool.

Mixing the two usually gives the most readable card: generate the artwork as an image, then have your coding assistant lay the stat line and provenance text around it. Generated images tend to mangle small text.

Validate cards.json

Five objects, each with the required keys. Same gate as the profile: it must parse.

Step 5 of 7

Session log and reflection

Graded independently of the artifacts it describes.

The log

What you asked, what came back, what you changed. Enough detail that a reader can follow how the profile and the cards were built.

W1 note: several logs were thin relative to the artifacts they described. If your log is shorter than the work it documents, it is not finished.

The reflection

A short section at the end of the log. You have just asked a generative tool to produce confident, attractive artifacts about actors whose attribution is contested, and in some cases disputed by the people accused.

Write down what that felt like, and what you would want a reader of those cards to know that the cards themselves do not say.

Ground this in Anderson Ch. 2. The chapter is about cognitive exploitation, authority compliance, and why people believe things that are presented well. Your cards are a specimen of exactly that: a polished card with a stat line and a MITRE ID reads as more authoritative than the same claim in a sentence, even though the evidence behind it has not changed.

Address at least: what did the card format add that the source did not support, and which Anderson concepts explain why it persuades past its evidence?

Module 9 returns to this question about AI in nuclear operations. Your answer here is the start of that thread, so write it honestly rather than tidily.
Step 6 of 7

Role synthesis

Secondary repo. One page. role-analyses/week-02/synthesis.md

This module's two lenses

Nation-State Threat Analyst

Thinks in external actors, technical TTPs, and attribution. Asks which actors are targeting this sector and what historical evidence says about their next move.

Insider Threat Investigator

Thinks in organisational psychology, behavioural baselines, and the limits of technical detection. Asks what conditions create insider risk and what indicators are actually observable.

Use the assigned roles. Several W1 submissions substituted different roles because the old template listed its examples with "e.g." That template has been corrected. For W2 the roles are the two above.

Apply both to your own profile and cards, not to the reading in general. The divergence to look for is not about facts. Both roles read the same reporting. It is about what each would actually do with it.

Anderson grounding, required. The Insider Threat Investigator lens must ground its behavioural-indicator claims in Anderson Ch. 2, citing at least two specific concepts from the chapter. Name them. "Anderson discusses psychology" is not a citation.

That chapter is the investigator's entire evidentiary basis: what conditions create insider risk, and which indicators are actually observable rather than merely plausible. The threat analyst works from technical TTPs and attribution. The investigator works from this. That difference in evidence base is where the two roles genuinely diverge.

Being straight with you about why this is here: across all eight W1 submissions, three of you never cited Anderson once, against dozens of NIST and RG 5.71 references each. The textbook is carrying no weight yet.
